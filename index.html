What still needs your hands

Drift feel
The measurable targets pass — drifting clears a 90° corner in 40 ticks against 55 for steering. What a machine can't judge is whether it's satisfying, and whether a first-timer can hold a slide without spinning.
Whether you can feel the sand pack
The stopwatch says yes — 2.8 seconds over three solo laps, measured against a control run with packing switched off. But the question is whether you notice the ground firming up under you, or just find lap three mysteriously easier.
Gamepads
Mapping and deadzone are verified against a stub, but no real controller has ever been plugged in. Analog steering may well want a different turn rate to the keyboard's.

Good to know

Out in the sandbox, the sand is the point. Loose sand is slow and vague; driving packs it down, and packed sand is nearly as quick as road. The racing line isn't painted on the track — you wear it in, and it resets every race. Try a time trial: alone, the line takes all three laps to form.

Dresser Drop is the first track that leaves the floor: a loop, then up onto the dresser, along the top, then down and back under the bit you were just on. Watch the gap between your car and its shadow — on the raised deck the shadow sits on the deck, not the carpet, so it still means what it always meant.

Shelf Run is the tall one — three storeys, so you drive under the shelf and then over it. There's no rail on the outside of the high corner. Run wide up there and you'll go over the edge, watch your car drop onto the carpet, and get put back on the track about a second down. The inside line is always safe, so it costs you nothing if you don't ask for it.

There's a loop on the floor and a two-turn corkscrew along the top of the dresser. Neither takes you unless you arrive fast enough — too slow and it doesn't grab you or punish you, you just carry on underneath and watch it go by. Arrive quickly and the ride is shorter, so both are worth carrying speed into. The loop pitches you over backwards; the corkscrew rolls you.

Each sandbox track picks a different fight with that. Dune Dash is wide and open. The Big Dig is a trench with nowhere else to be. Bucket Brigade puts buckets in the road with a spade propped up before each one — carry enough speed and it launches you clean over. Tide Pool floods the line, and you cannot wear a groove through water, so it arrives in pieces you have to keep re-finding.

Opponents share your physics exactly. They produce the same inputs a keyboard does and run through the same controller, so they cannot do anything your car can't — and that includes reading the sand. They can see packed ground because it's drawn on screen, same as you, so a line you wear in is a line they'll happily borrow. The Technician looks hardest; the Rookie hasn't noticed.

P or Esc pauses — sound, display and auto-accelerate live in there, along with restart and quit.

Up to four players share the keyboard, or plug in gamepads: seats claim them in order, so two pads and two keyboard players works. Two or more players is an exhibition — no medals, stars or ghosts, though toy pieces still count.
